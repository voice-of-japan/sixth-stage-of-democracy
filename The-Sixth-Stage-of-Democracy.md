# The Sixth Stage of Democracy

## From Institutions to Protocols

### A Proposal for Verifiable Presence and Physical Autonomy in the Age of Artificial Intelligence

**By Otoya Aizawa (Japan)**

# Abstract

For more than two centuries, democracy has largely been understood as a mechanism for determining who should govern.

Monarchs were replaced by property owners. Property owners were replaced by elected representatives. Representatives increasingly delegated authority to bureaucratic institutions. Today, those institutions are often influenced by global corporations, digital platforms, algorithms, and artificial intelligence.

Despite these transformations, one assumption has remained remarkably consistent:

**Someone must rule.**

This paper explores a different possibility.

What if the next stage of democratic evolution is not about selecting better rulers, but about creating social structures in which no individual, institution, corporation, state, or artificial intelligence can permanently become a ruler?

To examine this possibility, I propose a six-stage model of democratic evolution and introduce two conceptual frameworks:

* Verifiable Presence Protocol (VPP)
* Verifiable Physical Autonomy Protocol (VPAP)

These proposals are not presented as finished solutions. They are intended as hypotheses for criticism, discussion, experimentation, and further development.

# The Central Question

The history of democracy can be interpreted as a gradual expansion of political participation.

Yet each expansion has largely preserved the same underlying structure: power remains concentrated somewhere.

The question proposed here is therefore not:

**Who should rule?**

but rather:

**How can society be organized so that permanent domination becomes structurally impossible?**

The Sixth Stage is an attempt to explore that question.

# The Six Stages of Democratic Evolution

## Stage One: Privileged Democracy

Democracy emerged through resistance to absolute monarchy, but participation was initially restricted to privileged groups. The prosperity of early democratic societies often relied on colonial extraction, slavery, and unequal access to rights.

Universal principles coexisted with exclusion.

## Stage Two: Redistribution Democracy

The twentieth century demonstrated both the limits of centralized socialism and the instability of unrestricted capitalism.

Liberal democracies adapted by combining markets with redistribution through public education, healthcare, taxation, and social welfare systems.

This balance prolonged democratic stability.

## Stage Three: Globalized Democracy

Following the Cold War, capital increasingly transcended national borders.

Economic power became concentrated in global networks that often operated beyond the effective control of democratic institutions.

Political participation remained formally intact while practical influence became increasingly unequal.

## Stage Four: Digital Authoritarianism

As data replaced physical production as a primary source of value, new forms of power emerged.

Governments and digital platforms gained unprecedented abilities to monitor, predict, and influence behavior.

Participation remained visible, but autonomy gradually weakened.

The more society depended on algorithmic systems, the more human judgment risked becoming secondary.

## Stage Five: Information Sovereignty

Stage Five represents a defensive response.

Individuals seek to reclaim control over attention, identity, memory, and privacy.

Anonymity, temporary participation, and the right to be forgotten become increasingly important.

The goal is not withdrawal from society, but protection from continuous extraction and surveillance.

## Stage Six: Protocol Democracy

The Sixth Stage represents a transition from institutions toward protocols.

Rather than asking who should exercise power, the focus shifts toward limiting how power can be exercised.

Its core principle is simple:

**No one should be able to permanently become the ruler.**

Governments, corporations, experts, and artificial intelligence all remain capable of contributing to society, but none should possess irreversible authority.

Power must remain fragmented, transparent, auditable, and reversible.

# Verifiable Presence Protocol (VPP)

The Verifiable Presence Protocol explores how meaningful participation can remain tied to actual human presence while preserving anonymity.

In digital environments, influence can easily be amplified through automation, coordinated manipulation, and artificial engagement.

VPP seeks to establish mechanisms through which participation is linked to verifiable presence rather than accumulated power.

The objective is not identity verification, but participation verification.

Legitimacy emerges from demonstrated presence rather than institutional status, wealth, or technological advantage.

The precise implementation remains open for experimentation.

# Verifiable Physical Autonomy Protocol (VPAP)

Artificial intelligence introduces a challenge unlike previous concentrations of power.

Human cognitive capacity is limited. AI capabilities may continue to expand.

Attempts to regulate intelligence itself may ultimately prove ineffective.

VPAP therefore focuses on physical consequences rather than internal reasoning.

The central idea is that actions affecting the physical world should pass through transparent and verifiable constraints.

These constraints may apply to:

* Critical infrastructure
* Communication systems
* Energy networks
* Public services
* Automated decision systems

The goal is not to restrict knowledge or innovation.

The goal is to ensure that increasing intelligence does not translate into unlimited control over human beings.

# Remaining Challenges

## ## Defining Human Dignity

A central challenge concerns the definition of the values that protocols are intended to protect.

If governments, corporations, experts, or permanent institutions hold exclusive authority over these definitions, power may once again become concentrated.

One possible approach is the use of continuously rotating civic assemblies selected through sortition.

An important practical inspiration can be found in Japan's lay judge (saiban-in) system.

In this system, ordinary citizens participate temporarily in significant judicial decisions and then return to their everyday lives. Authority is exercised for a limited period and does not become a permanent status.

This principle may provide a useful reference for future democratic protocols. Citizens can assume responsibility without becoming a permanent governing class.

AI may assist by organizing information.

Experts may provide technical evaluation.

Protocols may enforce procedural safeguards.

However, final normative judgments should remain in human hands.

The participants responsible for such judgments should continuously change over time, ensuring that no individual, institution, or interest group can permanently accumulate authority.

The objective is not to identify perfect decision-makers, but to prevent the emergence of permanent rulers.

## Distributed Resilience

No protocol can eliminate the possibility of attack or failure.

Future democratic systems should therefore avoid dependence on single authorities, centralized infrastructures, or irreplaceable nodes.

Resilience requires decentralization and redundancy.

## Structural Forgetting

Modern surveillance derives power from permanent memory.

Human societies, however, also depend upon forgetting.

A democratic future may require mechanisms that allow information to expire naturally rather than accumulate indefinitely.

# Why I Reached This Conclusion

I am neither a philosopher nor a political theorist.

I am an ordinary citizen of Japan.

My perspective emerged through years of involvement with the aftermath of the Fukushima nuclear disaster.

There I witnessed how large systems respond when confronted with failure, responsibility, and uncertainty.

Institutions often prioritize continuity and self-preservation.

Human dignity can become secondary to operational necessity.

These experiences led me to question not only who governs, but whether existing structures of governance are sufficient for the challenges ahead.

As a practical experiment, I currently operate a small project called Reclaim Flowers (RF), which explores the preservation of three human qualities:

* Will
* Meaning
* Forgetting

RF is not a solution.

It is an attempt to explore whether certain human spaces can remain resistant to systems of extraction, optimization, and surveillance.

# Conclusion

The history of democracy has largely been a history of deciding who should rule.

The Sixth Stage asks a different question:

**How can we design systems in which nobody can permanently rule?**

The ideas presented here are incomplete and undoubtedly contain weaknesses.

They are offered not as doctrine, but as a hypothesis.

Their value, if any, depends on criticism, testing, refinement, and collective discussion.

If democracy is to survive the age of artificial intelligence, it may need to evolve beyond institutions alone and toward protocols that constrain power itself.
